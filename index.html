The Central Limit Theorem, by experiment

Why do averages always make a bell?

Professor Ada's research team collects messy data: dice rolls, yes/no survey answers, waiting times at a help desk. Every population looks different. Yet whenever they average a sample and repeat that many times, the same bell shape appears. Your mission is to find out why, and when it fails.

The theorem in one breath

Take many samples of size n from a population with mean μ and standard deviation σ. If the observations are independent and σ is finite, then for large enough n the sample means are approximately normally distributed:

x ≈ N( μ , σ / √n )

Centre: the true mean μ. Spread (the standard error): σ/√n. It is about the averages, not the raw data.

    Part 4

    Why does it behave like that?

    1. Highs and lows cancel

    An average mixes values together. One unusually large value in a sample is usually sitting next to ordinary or small ones, so its effect is diluted. Getting an extreme average needs every value in the sample to be extreme in the same direction, which becomes very unlikely as n grows.

    2. The middle has more ways to happen

    Roll two dice. A total of 2 can happen in only one way (1 + 1), but a total of 7 can happen in six ways. With more dice the count of combinations piles up even more in the middle, and the pile takes on the bell shape. The figure shows this counting for one, two and three dice.

    3. The spread shrinks like √n

    For independent values, variances add. The sum of n values has variance nσ², so the average has variance nσ²/n² = σ²/n. Take the square root and you get the standard error σ/√n. Four times more data only halves the uncertainty.

    Bachelor's level: why exactly the normal shape?

    Adding independent variables means convolving their distributions, and each convolution smooths the shape. After standardising, the skewness of x shrinks like γ/√n and the excess kurtosis like κ/n, so every feature that makes a distribution special fades away. The normal distribution is the only finite-variance shape that is unchanged by this “add and rescale” process, so it is where the process settles.

    That also explains why a strongly skewed population needs bigger samples. The exponential distribution has skewness 2, so at n = 30 the means still have skewness 2/√30 ≈ 0.37. The popular “n ≥ 30” rule is a rough habit, not a law.

    Master's level: the proof idea in four lines

    Let Y = (X − μ)/σ with characteristic function φY(t) = 1 − t²/2 + o(t²). For Zn = √n (x − μ)/σ = Σ Yi/√n, independence gives

    φZn(t) = [ φY(t/√n) ]n = [ 1 − t²/(2n) + o(1/n) ]n → e−t²/2,

    which is the characteristic function of N(0, 1). Lévy's continuity theorem turns this into convergence in distribution. The step that needs finite variance is the second-order expansion: with heavy tails such as the Cauchy (φ(t) = e−|t|) the expansion fails, and indeed the mean of n Cauchy values is again Cauchy with the same scale.

    CLT or Law of Large Numbers? They answer different questions.

    Law of Large Numbers

    Where does the average go? As n grows, x gets closer and closer to μ. It says nothing about the shape of the errors along the way.

    Central Limit Theorem

    How does the average wobble around μ? The errors x − μ are roughly normal, with size σ/√n. That is what lets us put a margin of error on an estimate.

    Read the small print

    When the theorem works, and when it does not

    Independence

    Each observation should not influence the others. Measurements from the same patient, the same machine on the same shift, or neighbouring days in a time series are often correlated. Correlation changes the standard error (usually making it bigger than σ/√n).

    Advanced: CLTs exist for weakly dependent data (mixing sequences, martingale differences), but the variance must include the autocovariances, which is why time-series work uses HAC / Newey-West standard errors.

    Finite variance

    If extreme values are so common that the variance is infinite, averaging does not tame them. Try the Cauchy population in the lab: the means never settle, whatever n you choose.

    Advanced: sums of heavy-tailed variables converge instead to α-stable laws (the generalised CLT). Financial returns and insurance losses sometimes sit close to this territory.

    “Large enough” depends on the population

    There is no single sample size that works for every population. A symmetric population may look normal at n = 5; a strongly skewed one may need n in the hundreds; a rare yes/no event needs both np and n(1 − p) to be reasonably large (a common guide is at least 10).

    Bachelor's: the Berry–Esseen theorem gives a guaranteed bound on the error of the normal approximation, proportional to E|X − μ|³ / (σ³ √n).

    Identical distribution is not essential

    The classic version assumes every observation comes from the same population. Later versions allow different populations, as long as no single observation dominates the total.

    Advanced: this is the Lindeberg condition. Lyapunov's condition (a slightly higher moment) is a convenient sufficient check.

    Part 5

    How it was discovered

    Nobody discovered it in one go. It took about two centuries, moving from gamblers' coin problems to a general law of probability.

    1. 1733

      Abraham de Moivre

      A French mathematician living in London, working on gambling problems. He showed that the number of heads in many coin tosses follows a smooth bell curve, which made long binomial calculations practical. This is the first special case of the theorem.

    2. 1809–1812

      Carl Friedrich Gauss and Pierre-Simon Laplace

      Gauss used the normal curve for errors in astronomical measurements. Laplace then argued that a sum of many small independent errors should be approximately normal, whatever the individual errors look like, and set this out in his 1812 Théorie analytique des probabilités.

    3. 1870s–1889

      Francis Galton's bean machine

      Galton built a board where beads bounce left or right off pins and pile up in a bell shape. It made the idea visible to non-mathematicians; the 3D lab above is a descendant of that board.

    4. 1901

      Aleksandr Lyapunov

      Gave the first fully rigorous proof under general conditions, using characteristic functions. From here on the result was a theorem, not just a very good observation.

    5. 1920

      George Pólya names it

      Pólya called it the zentraler Grenzwertsatz, the “central limit theorem”. “Central” refers to its central importance in probability theory.

    6. 1922–1935

      Lindeberg, Lévy and Feller

      They pinned down exactly when the theorem holds (the Lindeberg condition) and when it cannot, completing the classical story.

    7. 1924 →

      From mathematics to factories

      Walter Shewhart, an engineer at Western Electric and later Bell Telephone Laboratories, designed control charts that track averages of small subgroups of products. Because averages are close to normal, simple limits of μ ± 3σ/√n could flag a process going wrong. Quality engineering still runs on this idea.

    Part 6

    What it is used for, and why modern research leans on it

    Most data analysis reports an estimate plus its uncertainty. The CLT is the reason a simple, shared recipe for that uncertainty works across fields as different as medicine, marketing and manufacturing.

    Academic research

    • Confidence intervals. x ± 1.96·SE gives a 95% interval for the true mean, even when the raw data are not normal.
    • Hypothesis tests. z-tests, t-tests, tests for proportions and much of ANOVA rely on test statistics that are approximately normal in large samples.
    • Surveys and polls. A poll of 1,000 people has a margin of error of about ±3 percentage points: 1.96 × √(0.5 × 0.5 / 1000) ≈ 0.031.
    • Regression and modelling. Standard errors and p-values for regression coefficients, logistic models and maximum likelihood estimates come from CLT-type results.
    • Clinical trials and meta-analysis. Differences in mean outcomes and pooled effect sizes are treated as approximately normal to judge whether a treatment works.
    • Simulation and the bootstrap. Monte Carlo estimates come with error bars of size σ/√n, and bootstrap methods are justified by the same asymptotic reasoning.

    Industrial research and practice

    • Statistical process control. X̄ control charts watch the averages of small product samples; limits at μ ± 3σ/√n separate normal variation from real faults.
    • A/B testing. Online companies compare conversion rates of two designs; with thousands of visitors per group the difference in rates is close to normal, so a z-test decides the winner.
    • Measurement and sensors. Averaging 16 readings from a noisy sensor cuts random noise by a factor of 4. Labs and IoT systems use this every day.
    • Reliability and quality engineering. Six Sigma capability studies, acceptance sampling and tolerance stack-ups all model sums and averages as normal.
    • Risk pooling. Insurers and supply chains are more predictable in total than per customer: the total of many independent demands or claims is close to normal and relatively less variable.
    • Machine learning evaluation. The accuracy measured on a test set is an average of 0/1 outcomes, so its confidence interval comes straight from the CLT.

    Why it matters so much

    Researchers almost never know the true shape of their population. The CLT says they often do not need to: if they work with averages (or sums, or many common estimators) from reasonably large, independent samples, one well-understood distribution describes the uncertainty. That is why the same formulas appear in a psychology paper, a drug approval file and a production-line dashboard. It is also why the assumptions deserve respect: dependent data, heavy tails and small samples are where real analyses go wrong.

    Master's corner

    Formal statements worth knowing

    Lindeberg–Lévy (i.i.d.)

    If X1, …, Xn are i.i.d. with E[X] = μ and 0 < Var(X) = σ² < ∞, then √n (x − μ)/σ → N(0, 1) in distribution.

    Lindeberg–Feller

    For independent, non-identical Xi with variances σi² and sn² = Σσi², the standardised sum is asymptotically normal if for every ε > 0, (1/sn²) Σ E[(Xi − μi)² 1{|Xi − μi| > ε sn}] → 0.

    Berry–Esseen

    With ρ = E|X − μ|³ < ∞: supx |Fn(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ C ρ / (σ³ √n), with a universal constant C below 0.48. The error shrinks like 1/√n.

    Slutsky and the t-statistic

    Replacing σ with the sample SD s changes nothing asymptotically because s → σ in probability. For small samples from normal data, the exact distribution is Student's t (Gosset, 1908).

    Multivariate CLT and the delta method

    √n (x − μ) → Nk(0, Σ) for vectors. For a smooth function g, √n (g(x) − g(μ)) → N(0, ∇g′ Σ ∇g), which gives standard errors for ratios, log odds and elasticities.

    Dependence and heavy tails

    Martingale and mixing CLTs cover time series with a long-run variance in place of σ². With infinite variance, suitably scaled sums converge to α-stable laws; the Cauchy is the α = 1 case.

    Reference

    Glossary

    Population
    Every value you could possibly observe. Its average is μ and its spread is σ.
    Sample
    The n values you actually collect, chosen at random.
    Sample mean (x)
    The average of one sample. Different samples give different x values.
    Sampling distribution
    The distribution you would see if you repeated the sampling many times and plotted every x. The CLT is a statement about this distribution.
    Standard deviation (σ)
    The typical distance of a single value from the population mean.
    Standard error (SE)
    The standard deviation of the sample mean: the typical distance of x from μ. Equal to σ/√n for independent observations.
    Normal distribution
    The symmetric bell curve. About 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the centre and 95% within 1.96 SD.
    Skewness
    A number for lopsidedness. 0 is symmetric; a long right tail gives a positive value.
    Independent
    Knowing one observation tells you nothing about another.
    Random seed
    The starting number for the computer's random generator. Same seed, same sequence of “random” draws.
